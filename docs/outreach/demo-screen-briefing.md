# Demo screen briefing

**Who this is for:** whoever is on the call with the caseload screen open. It
explains what is on the screen and what each part is there to provoke. The
questions themselves live in section 5 of
[`retention-interviews.md`](retention-interviews.md) — this document is what you
read to understand the answers.

You do not need to know how any of it was built. If a question goes past what is
here, "I don't know, I'll find out and come back to you" is a good answer and
costs nothing. Write the question down.

## You may well not show it at all

The demo is shown **only if the provider says they want to see it.** It is
offered once, near the end of the call, and anything short of a clear yes ends
it: hesitation, no time, "another time", or "send it over and I'll look". Thank
them and close. Do not ask twice, and do not send it afterwards instead.

This is not politeness, it is the method. Someone watching out of courtesy will
agree with whatever is on the screen, and agreement is the one thing the call is
designed not to collect. The twenty-five minutes before the offer are the real
interview; a call that ends without the demo has still done its job, and several
of them will.

So have it running and ready, and expect not to need it.

## What you are showing, and what it claims

Ontario pays employment-services providers when a client they placed is still
working an average of 20-plus hours a week at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after the
job started — and only when a document the funder accepts proves it. The screen
takes ordinary employment records and works out, checkpoint by checkpoint,
whether each one is payable and why.

The claim being tested is narrow: that providers routinely lose money on work
their clients genuinely did, because the documents on file do not cover it. If
that is not true, or not expensive, we want to hear so on this call.

**What it is not.** It does not score anyone, rank anyone, or decide who is
ready for work. Nothing on it writes, there is no sign-in, it runs on this
laptop, and the twelve people on it are invented. The employers are called
"Example Distribution Co." and so on for that reason.

time ago, checkpoints will have come due since and the counts drift upward.

pre-approval", for an attestation with no reference; the database refuses to
store that combination, so it cannot appear in the demo.

## Clicking a name

A client's name opens the reasoning behind their row, in four parts.

**Checkpoints** repeats the verdicts with a one-line basis under each, plus the
cumulative weeks worked and a plain statement of the one rule we had to guess.

**Employment periods and evidence** is the heart of it: each period of work with
its hours, its wage, the document covering it, and whether that document is
accepted (✓), acceptable but not on file (?), or not acceptable (✕). Periods are
kept separate rather than merged, because a document proves the period it covers
and no other.

**Follow-up contact** lists every attempt to reach the client, including the ones
that failed, at the same weight as the ones that worked. This is Farah Nasser's
point: she stopped answering at month four, three attempts are recorded, and
month 6 is lost anyway. Ask whether that effort has anywhere to go in CaMS today
or whether it simply vanishes.

**Recorded milestones** are written once, with the evidence as stated at the
time, so a later correction cannot silently rewrite a claim already submitted.

## The printable pack

The link at the top of a client page opens a funder submission — client and
placement, checkpoints claimed with the basis for each, the supporting
documents, the follow-up attempts, and a closing section that states its own
assumptions and limits. Print it with Ctrl+P, or save it as a PDF if they ask
for a copy.

The "Assumptions and limits" section is deliberate and is worth pointing at
rather than skipping. It says in the document itself that a document is judged
on its type and whether it is on file, and **not** on the five fields the funder
requires on the page; that self-report is treated as unacceptable; and that
hours worked can exceed hours evidenced. Naming the limits is what makes the
rest of the paper worth reading, and it invites the correction we want.

The strongest question on the call uses this pack: would your Service System
Manager accept *this piece of paper* as the submission, or must the milestone
originate in CaMS?

## How the screen relates to the questions

It is not there to show that we are right. It is there to get corrections that
would take months to find any other way — the fastest way to learn that a
verdict is wrong is to put it in front of someone who does the job and watch
them disagree.

So the framing is "tell me where this is wrong", never "isn't this useful". The
ask-by-ask mapping is the table in section 5 of
[`retention-interviews.md`](retention-interviews.md). Three rules from that guide
decide whether any of it is worth anything:

- **Show it only if they asked to see it.** Offered once, run on a clear yes,
  dropped on anything else.
- **Get their cost number before they see ours.** The middle card holds that
  exact quantity, so their own unprompted figure is only uncontaminated if it
  comes first.
- **Show nothing until the falsifying question has been asked and answered.** A
  milestone table shown early gets described back in our own vocabulary, and we
  would mistake our own framework repeated for agreement.

**Elif Kaya is the most valuable row on the page.** She worked, stopped, and
restarted; we score her month 3 as lost. No public directive says how hours
aggregate across a gap like that — we guessed, and the guess is on screen as a
stated assumption. A provider contradicting it is a success, not a setback, and
the cheapest correction available to us.

## Say these three things before they ask, not after

1. These are twelve synthetic people. Never call it pilot data.
2. Nothing writes. It is read-only and there is no sign-in.
3. It is running on this laptop, not a service anyone is signed up to.

## Questions you will get

| If they ask | Say |
| --- | --- |
| Does this connect to CaMS? | Not yet, and we do not know whether it would be permitted. **Do not promise it.** |
| Can we try it? Can I log in? | No. Nothing writes and there is no sign-in yet. |
| Is this real client data? | No, all twelve are invented. |
| What is a checkpoint worth? | Those weightings are not published, which is why no dollar figure appears anywhere on the screen. Ask your Service System Manager for your contract's numbers. |
| What does it cost? What are you selling? | There is no price. This call is to find out whether the problem is real and worth solving. |
| Who else is using it? | Nobody. This is one of the first four conversations. |
| Anything about how the rules were derived | Point at the assumptions section, and offer to send [`ontario-outcome-framework.md`](ontario-outcome-framework.md), which lists every source and every open question. |

If the conversation reaches client privacy — and one provider told researchers
they had lost clients over demanding proof of employment — the honest position
is that per-recipient consent and an access log exist, that they sit on the
person-facing views only, and that nothing on the provider screens surfaces
consent or logs anything. Do not describe it as solved.

## What not to click

Stay on **Provider caseload** and the client pages underneath it. The top
navigation also offers Intake, Track, Attestor, Share view, Accommodation, and
Access log; those render the passport, levels, and health-check surfaces this
call is deliberately staying away from. The round button in the bottom-left
corner is the development toolbar — ignore it.

## What to write down

The tracker at the end of [`retention-interviews.md`](retention-interviews.md)
has a column per thing we need. Two are worth more than the rest:

- **Their words for the middle number.** Whatever they call "earned but not
  provable" internally is the phrase to use in every later conversation.
- **Any verdict they say is wrong.** Verbatim, without defending the screen. Two
  providers calling the same verdict wrong is more important than whether they
  would buy it.
