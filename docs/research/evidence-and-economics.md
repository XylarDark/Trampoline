# Trampoline: Evidence Base and Buyer Economics

> **Compiled 2026-09-07 by web research.** Every factual claim below is linked to a source URL that was actually returned in search results. Figures were transcribed from search result content and page text; **they should be re-verified against the primary source before being used in a funding application, investor deck, or any public claim.** Several figures are dated (some pre-2015), several are from vendor marketing rather than primary research, and these are flagged explicitly in the "Evidence gaps and weak sources" section. Nothing in this document has been softened to favour the product.

**Scope:** Ontario, Canada. Early-stage workforce product ("Trampoline") — a readiness passport in which partners (clinics, gyms/physio, counselors, schools, employment agencies, employers) issue standardized pass/fail/restricted attestations; the system stores only verdicts, restrictions and expiry dates, computes staged readiness levels (Stabilize, Capacity, Trainable, Employable, 90-day Hold), and lets employers and training programs publish requirements as gates that unlock seats and jobs.

**Core claim under test:** staged, partner-verified readiness reduces hiring and training risk — fewer no-shows, fewer early dropouts, better retention — and is therefore worth paying for.

---

## 1. Bottom line

- **The gating premise is the specific thing ~28 RCTs and two Cochrane reviews were run to disprove.** Rapid placement beat pre-vocational preparation 34% vs 12% employed at 18 months, and prevocational training did not even beat standard community care. Selling staged gating into Ontario's disability employment system means selling against its own stated best practice.
- **Readiness assessment has been tested and found not to predict success.** IPS's "zero exclusion" rule exists precisely because of this; its practice manual states that "practitioners are unable to predict who will be successful at work." The clinical instruments underneath (functional capacity evaluations) show "poor" predictive efficiency for sustained return to work.
- **Better information does not change employer behaviour toward this population.** In a Canadian field experiment, a high-quality verified signal raised callbacks 10 points for everyone and left the 25-point disability gap intact. Separately, telling employers the government would pay for accommodation did not move callbacks either.
- **The barrier you are solving is not the barrier people report.** The top hiring-stage barrier for Canadians with disabilities is *disclosing* disability (54%), not proving capability. Trampoline is structurally a disclosure instrument. Meanwhile the ODSP 75% clawback above $1,000/month drives documented, deliberate underworking — and collides head-on with Employment Ontario's 20-hour funding threshold.
- **There is a real product in here, but it is not the one in the brief.** Clinician-to-workplace contact and accommodation offers are among the only interventions with *strong* evidence for cutting work-disability duration; MDRC found individualized *routing* beats universal gating; and Ontario providers are already staffing entire "retention departments" just to capture proof of employment. Pivot the pitch to routing, accommodation specification, and outcome-evidence infrastructure.

## 2. Per-question findings

### Q1. Does staging or sequencing actually work? (IPS vs. train-then-place)

**The evidence is strongly against gating. This is not close, and it is the most important finding in this brief.**

The Cochrane review of vocational rehabilitation for severe mental illness identified 18 RCTs of reasonable quality and found supported employment (rapid placement) significantly outperformed pre-vocational training: at 18 months, **34% employed under supported employment versus 12% under pre-vocational training** (RR random effects for unemployment 0.76, 95% CI 0.64–0.89, NNT 4.5). Clients in supported employment also earned more and worked more hours per month. Critically, the review also concluded there was **"no evidence that Pre-vocational Training was more effective in helping clients to obtain competitive employment than standard community care"** — the preparation phase did not beat doing nothing ([Cochrane CD003080 plain-language summary](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD003080_vocational-rehabilitation-people-severe-mental-illness); [full review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4170889/)).

The later Cochrane network meta-analysis reached the same conclusion across 18 trials with short-term follow-up (N = 2,291): supported employment was more effective than prevocational training (**RR 2.52, 95% CI 1.21–5.24**) and than transitional employment (**RR 3.49, 95% CI 1.77–6.89**) in obtaining competitive employment. Prevocational training itself was **not considerably different from psychiatric care only** (**RR 1.26, 95% CI 0.73–2.19**; SUCRA 40.3, very low-quality evidence) ([Cochrane CD011867](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD011867_helping-adults-severe-mental-illness-get-job-and-keep-it-network-meta-analysis); [summary](https://copratique.santementaletravail.ca/interventions-for-obtaining-and-maintaining-employment-in-adults-with-severe-mental-illness-a-network-meta-analysis/)).

The IPS Employment Center reports **28 randomized controlled trials, all showing a significant advantage for IPS**, with an average competitive employment rate of **55% for IPS versus 25% for controls**, and a meta-analysis of 17 RCTs finding people receiving IPS were **2.4 times more likely to be employed** than controls. Across four RCTs over 18 months, approximately **three times as many** IPS recipients achieved employment and they worked **four times as many hours** ([IPS Employment Center](https://ipsworks.org/index.php/evidence-for-ips/)).

A separate PLOS One meta-analysis of IPS RCTs found significant advantages for obtaining any competitive employment (**RR 1.63, 95% CI 1.46–1.82**), job tenure (**d = 0.55, 95% CI 0.33–0.79**), job length (d = 0.46) and income (d = 0.48), while non-vocational outcomes (quality of life, global functioning, mental health) included the null. The authors describe the contrast succinctly: **"SE/IPS is 'place-and-train/treat' whereas TAU is 'train/treat-then-place'"** ([PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0212208)).

Contemporary trials continue to replicate this. A 2025 RCT in a high-unemployment setting found IPS participants achieved significantly higher rates of competitive employment and greater improvements in global functioning than train-then-place vocational rehabilitation ([Int J Public Health](https://doi.org/10.3389/ijph.2025.1608796)). A Scandinavian multicenter RCT explicitly compared IPS against a control condition of "stepwise approaches including prevocational training and non-competitive work," including sheltered business traineeships "where the work capability of the clients is tested" ([Scand J Work Environ Health](https://doi.org/10.5271/sjweh.3753)).

**The most damaging finding for Trampoline specifically is not the effect size — it is the mechanism.** IPS's "zero exclusion" principle exists *because readiness assessment was found not to work*:

- MDRC's review states that "IPS programs differ from many employment programs in that there is no systematic focus on developing 'job readiness' skills. The philosophy of IPS is that the focus should be on immediate job search (so long as the client wants it), and that starting job search and making contact with employers on a client's behalf **should not depend on an assessment of readiness** by the program or the employment specialist." Clients should have first face-to-face contact with an employer **within 30 days** of program entry ([MDRC](https://www.mdrc.org/work/publications/individual-placement-and-support/file-full)).
- The IPS practice manual is blunter: **"practitioners are unable to predict who will be successful at work,"** and programs "avoid developing work readiness programs because they understand that using a stepwise approach to employment is counter to IPS practice principles." It adds: **"In IPS, assessments are not used to determine whether youth are ready to work"** ([IPS for youth manual, UMass](https://www.umassmed.edu/contentassets/32a28fea786c4a62a8f3e47242ebaf1e/ips-se-for-youth_print-prepub-final.pdf)).
- The Scandinavian Journal of Disability Research describes zero exclusion as "**no eligibility criteria or job readiness screening** and no requirement for rehabilitation before referral to the service," because "an individual's interest in working has shown to be a more significant predictor of success than assessments of job readiness conducted by professionals" ([SJDR](https://sjdr.se/articles/10.16993/sjdr.1008)).
- Empirically, people with *no initial interest in work* still achieved competitive employment at rates comparable to motivated candidates when given vocational services; the authors concluded that "receptivity to work can be improved through the receipt of vocational services, and, therefore, reported (dis)interest **is not a justifiable inclusion (or exclusion) criterion**" ([Work Interest as a Predictor of Competitive Employment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4636005/)).
- Zero exclusion is now being applied beyond severe mental illness, to people with combined physical, psychological and social limitations in welfare-to-work services ([RCT of IPS and participatory workplace intervention](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12360975/)).

A mixed-methods umbrella review confirms IPS as the most extensively studied supported employment model, with demonstrated cost-effectiveness ([umbrella review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11152269/)). A historical note worth internalizing: prior to IPS, approaches "shared the assumption that patients with serious mental illness were not ready for employment and needed training and other prevocational experiences before entering competitive jobs" — approaches that "had little or no rigorous empirical support" ([Individual placement and support: History, current status, and future directions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11114326/)).

**The one genuine nuance in favour of sequencing.** In the US National Evaluation of Welfare-to-Work Strategies (NEWWS), MDRC randomly assigned over 15,000 welfare recipients from 1991–1994 to labor force attachment (job-search-first, LFA), human capital development (education-first, HCD), or control. Both increased earnings versus control. Directly compared, **initial assignment to job search increased earnings by about $300 more per year** than initial assignment to basic education in the first two years. The education-first advantage that appeared 10–15 years later was **not statistically significant**. LFA was also "much cheaper to operate" ([MDRC brief](https://www.mdrc.org/work/publications/job-search-or-basic-education-participation-first); [full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED570522.pdf); [alternate full text](https://www.mdrc.org/work/publications/job-search-or-basic-education-participation-first/file-full); [NEWWS executive summary](https://www.mdrc.org/sites/default/files/execsum_19.pdf)).

But MDRC's cross-study synthesis adds the qualifier that matters most for Trampoline: **"The most effective programs fell in the middle of the spectrum. In these programs, some recipients started by looking for work, while others started with education or training. This finding suggests that a more individualized approach may be most promising,"** with the caution that "the types of services provided and **the basis on which people are assigned to services** appear to be also critical." Job-search-first programs also "led to larger gains for more disadvantaged groups, such as people without a high school credential" ([MDRC](https://www.mdrc.org/work/publications/how-welfare-and-work-policies-affect-employment-and-income)).

**That is the single most defensible foothold for Trampoline in the entire literature — and it supports *routing*, not *gating*.**

### Q2. Dropout and attrition in Canadian/Ontario employment and training programs

Real and large numbers are available here.

**Apprenticeship completion (Ontario):**

- **Ontario apprenticeship completion averaged about 47%** across 2011/12–2015/16, splitting **35% for voluntary trades versus 59% for compulsory trades**. The Auditor General also found that among employers who had sponsored at least 50 apprentices, approximately **100 employers had completion rates under 20%** yet were still actively training almost **4,800 apprentices** ([Auditor General of Ontario, 3.04 Employment Ontario](https://auditor.on.ca/en/content/annualreports/arreports/en16/v1_304en16.pdf)).
- HEQCO, using the 2015 National Apprenticeship Survey, found Ontario completion rates ranging from **about 54% in the industrial trade group to more than 67% in the construction trades** ([HEQCO, The Journey of Ontario Apprentices](https://heqco.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Formatted_Journey-of-Ontario-Apprentices-FINAL.pdf)).
- Variation by trade is dramatic: approximately **81% for plumbing, 32% for welding, 31% for food services, and 33% for machinist** ([HEQCO, Diving into the Trades](https://heqco.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Diving-Into-The-Trades-FINAL.pdf)).

**Apprenticeship completion (national):**

- **About two in five apprentices discontinue their programs within 1.5 times program duration** — more than the share who complete and become certified ([Statistics Canada, Discontinuers of apprenticeship programs](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/81-595-m/81-595-m2024001-eng.htm)).
- The certification rate within *expected* program duration was **20.1% in 2023**, up 0.9 percentage points from 2022 but still below the 2019 level of **21.8%** ([Statistics Canada, The Daily](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/241211/dq241211c-eng.htm)). Underlying series: [Statistics Canada Table 37-10-0193-01](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=3710019301).

**Ontario pre-apprenticeship — the contractual benchmark:**

Ontario's Pre-Apprenticeship Training Program already sets an explicit target: **"It is expected that a minimum of 80% of program participants will successfully complete the program (program completion rate)"** and **"a minimum of 80% of program participants who successfully complete the program will become registered apprentices or obtain employment in the skilled trades (post-program completion rate)."** The program completion rate is defined as participants enrolled on day 1 versus those completing all mandatory activities. Providers must report the **number of participants who exited the project early for unknown/other reasons**, and post-program data is collected at **3, 6 and 12 months**. Achievement of these rates "will be considered by the ministry when evaluating future applications for funding" ([MLITSD Pre-Apprenticeship Training Program Call for Proposals 2026-27](https://forms.mgcs.gov.on.ca/en/dataset/1c808860-adfb-451b-a3fe-3e9a82681e18/resource/4dfa3831-e533-4d0a-9983-45caa0ec4e0b/download/pre-apprenticeship-2026-27-guidelines-en-final.pdf)).

**This is your ready-made benchmark and your most likely first buyer.**

**Why people actually drop out — note that none of it is "unverified readiness":**

- HEQCO found participants who gave a reason for non-completion **"overwhelmingly indicated that they discontinued their training for financial reasons"** — EI waiting periods too long, failure to qualify for EI, or pay rates felt incompatible with their education/experience. Cited literature reasons include lack of knowledge about the apprenticeship process, differing expectations between employers and apprentices, and poor employability skills ([HEQCO, The Apprentice Retention Program](https://heqco.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Apprentice_Retention_Prog-ENG.pdf)).
- A Hamilton-area study found learning challenges during in-school training, with participants "without a high school diploma or with a learning disability" struggling, and **several reporting they "didn't know about learning supports or accommodations available"** ([ABEA Apprenticeship Research Report](https://lchamilton.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/ABEAApprenticeshipResearchReport-Final-Feb2024.pdf)).

**Social assistance recidivism (dated, local):** approximately **52% in Waterloo Region, with almost 69% returning to Ontario Works within 9 months** ([Literacy Network Ontario / LBS info document](https://lbsresourcesandforum.contactnorth.ca/pluginfile.php/583/mod_data/content/2014/LNO-LBSInfoDoc-Jan2020.pdf), citing Region of Waterloo 2011). Same source: in 2018-19, 30% of LBS learners were employed; of learners completing service, 30% reported being employed and 23% went on to further education/training.

**No strong evidence found:** I could not locate a published province-wide *no-show* rate for Ontario employment programs. Do not claim one.

### Q3. Employer cost of turnover, no-shows, and early attrition

**Honest warning: this is the weakest evidence base in the brief.** Nearly everything available in a Canadian framing is vendor marketing content recycling US benchmarks (SHRM, Gallup, US Department of Labor) rather than primary Canadian research. Treat these as directional only and expect a sophisticated buyer to challenge them.

**Replacement cost as a share of salary:**

- Entry-level/non-skilled: **30–50% of annual salary**; technical and supervisor roles 100–150% ([G&A Partners](https://www.gnapartners.com/resources/articles/how-much-does-employee-turnover-really-cost-your-business)).
- Frontline workers average around **40%**, with a typical dollar range of **$8,000–$20,000**; entry-level/hourly 30–50% at $3,500–$10,000 ([FirstHR](https://firsthr.app/blog/onboarding/cost-of-employee-turnover)).
- A Canadian source citing SHRM and the Work Institute puts frontline replacement at **30–50% of annual salary** and suggests a working number of **6 to 9 months of total compensation** for an experienced hourly worker. For a $55,000/year warehouse lead in Ontario, that is **roughly $16,000 to $30,000** all-in ([Canuck Hire](https://canuckhire.com/blog/employee-turnover-costs-canadian-smbs)).

**Cost per hire and time to fill:**

- **~$5,475 average cost per hire and a 44-day median time to fill** (SHRM benchmarks as cited). Applying Mercer's Canadian turnover figures — **11.9% average, 25.9% for retail/wholesale**, the highest of any sector and the category covering most warehouse work — a 100-person warehouse should expect ~26 replacements a year, **roughly $142,000/year in recruiting cost alone**, before lost productivity, supervisor time, or learning-curve errors ([Trimax, Warehouse Staffing in Canada](https://trimaxemployment.ca/warehouse-staffing-canada-guide/)).
- An alternate figure of **US$4,700 average cost per hire, up 14% from 2019** (SHRM), with agency fees at 15–25% of annual salary ([FirstHR](https://firsthr.app/blog/onboarding/cost-of-employee-turnover)).

**Cost of a failed hire:**

- The **US Department of Labor** estimate of **up to 30% of the employee's first-year earnings**, and **Gallup's** estimate of one-half to two times annual salary (~40% for a frontline role). For a **$40,000/year line worker**, this frames a realistic all-in bad-hire cost of **$16,000 to $30,000**, roughly 40–70% of annual pay, once re-recruiting, lost output, gap cover (overtime at 1.5x and premium temp labour), quality costs (scrap, rework, mispicks, returns), safety/claim risk and morale contagion are included ([Trimax, The Real Cost of a Bad Hire in Light Industrial](https://trimaxemployment.ca/real-cost-of-a-bad-hire-light-industrial-canada/)).
- Applied to a $45,000 warehouse role, the 30%-of-first-year-earnings figure yields **roughly $13,500** ([Trimax](https://trimaxemployment.ca/warehouse-staffing-canada-guide/)).

**No strong evidence found:** no credible primary-source Canadian figure for **first-90-days attrition cost** specifically. If this number matters to your pitch, generate it from a partner employer's own payroll and scheduling data rather than cite a secondary source.

### Q4. Return-to-work economics in Ontario

This is the strongest funder-facing evidence in the brief — and it is favourable, but it favours *coordination*, not *gating*.

**Current WSIB baseline:**

- In 2024, WSIB helped **87.5% of people return to work within three months** of a workplace injury or illness. **The average time someone was off work is now down to 61.4 days, about a full week shorter than two years earlier.** WSIB attributes to these improvements **"significant savings of over $100 million in benefit costs since 2021."** Total claim payments in 2024 were **$2,640 million**, $30 million (1.1%) under budget; loss-of-earnings costs were 3% below budget. Time to first decision: 96% within 10 business days; time to first payment: 82% within 15 business days ([WSIB 2024 Annual Report](https://www.wsib.ca/sites/default/files/2025-11/2024_annual_report_20250429_final_with_signatures.pdf)).

**Historical proof that process redesign moves these numbers:**

- After WSIB's New Service Delivery Model (introduced 2008–2009), the percentage of workers still on workers' compensation benefits after 12 months **fell from 8.9% in 2009 to 3.9% by early 2013** (it had more than doubled from 4.3% in 1999 to 8.9% in 2009 before the reform). Claim eligibility decisions within two weeks rose from **65% in 2008 to 90% by early 2013**. Workers returning to work within a year of injury without wage loss rose from **85% in 2008 to 92%**. A comparison of annual benefit expenditures between 2009 and 2012 documented a **reduction in loss-of-earnings benefits of $181 million** — though WSIB noted this was in part attributable to a decline in the number of accepted lost-time claims ([IWH impact case study](https://www.iwh.on.ca/impact-case-studies/evidence-based-service-delivery-model-at-wsib-improved-return-to-work-outcomes)).
- The 92% figure was sustained through 2015 ([IWH, WSIB integrates prognostic information](https://www.iwh.on.ca/impact-case-studies/wsib-integrates-prognostic-information-in-improved-return-to-work-case-management-services)). Context: approximately **40% of Ontario workers' compensation lost-time claims are attributed to soft-tissue injuries of the low back**.
- The Work Reintegration Program (2010), replacing Labour Market Re-entry: in its first two years, **69% of workers who completed their program returned to work, compared to 36% in 2009 under LMR**. Program costs fell from **$168 million in 2009 to $91 million in 2012** (a $77 million reduction). Injured worker satisfaction rose from **49% to 85%** ([IWH, WSIB work reintegration program](https://www.iwh.on.ca/impact-case-studies/wsib-work-reintegration-program-improves-outcomes-lowers-costs)).

**What actually shortens return to work — read this carefully:**

IWH's systematic review (initiated at WSIB's request; 4,124 papers screened, 10 of sufficient quality) found:

- **Strong evidence** that work disability duration is significantly reduced by **work accommodation offers** and **contact between healthcare provider and workplace**.
- **Moderate evidence** for **early contact with the worker by the workplace**, **ergonomic worksite visits**, and **presence of a return-to-work coordinator**.
- **Moderate evidence** that these five components reduce associated costs.
- Evidence for **sustainability** of these effects was "insufficient or limited," and evidence on quality-of-life impact was "insufficient or mixed."
- "Conditions of goodwill and mutual confidence are influential factors contributing to the success of RTW arrangements."

([IWH best-evidence summary](https://www.iwh.on.ca/summaries/sharing-best-evidence/workplace-based-return-to-work-interventions); [Journal of Occupational Rehabilitation](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10926-005-8038-8); [IWH journal article record](https://www.iwh.on.ca/journal-articles/workplace-based-return-to-work-interventions-systematic-review-of-quantitative-literature); [IWH full systematic review report](https://iwh.on.ca/sites/iwh/files/iwh/reports/iwh_sys_review_rtw_interventions_2004_overview.pdf))

**Every validated lever is about connecting the workplace to the clinician early and offering modified work. None of them is "certify the worker as ready before they may proceed."**

**On urgency:** WSIB's own literature review notes that **"After 12 weeks off work, the risk of prolonged work absence increases dramatically and by 6 months, only 50% of workers ever return to full-time employment"** (citing ACOEM 2006). It also notes that early re-employment "is more conducive than a later return to work to resolving depressive symptoms resulting from job loss" ([WSIB, Better at Work](https://www.wsib.ca/sites/default/files/2022-11/betteratwork.pdf)).

**Counterweight:** a 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs of work-related interventions for people on sick leave found no significant difference between multidisciplinary rehabilitation and usual care (**RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.70–1.48** at 12 months) or between MR and other active interventions (**RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.86–1.25**), noting that "studies have struggled to show positive long-term effects" ([Systematic Reviews](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13643-022-02055-7)). Do not overclaim the RTW intervention literature.

### Q5. What Ontario workforce funders actually measure and pay for

The mechanics are unusually well documented and highly specific. A vendor that cannot speak in these exact units will not be credible.

**ODSP Employment Supports payment structure:**

- Payment triggers on a **13-cumulative-week job placement**, after which a job retention fee is earned **for each month the client is employed, for up to 33 consecutive months for ODSP income support recipients and 15 consecutive months for clients not receiving ODSP income support**.
- For ODSP income support recipients, the job retention payment is **60% of the client's chargeable earnings per month of competitive employment**, with a **minimum payment of $250 per month for the first 15 months**. Chargeable earnings are determined by applying a 50% earnings exemption to net earnings and deducting eligible childcare and disability-related expenses.
- For clients not receiving ODSP income support, the retention payment is a flat **$250 per month** for 15 consecutive months.
- **"Job retention funding is reconciled based on targets achieved (outcome-based)."** Funding flows monthly in advance of outcomes but "is earned when the service provider is successful in meeting their agreed upon targets," and is "subject to reconciliation and recovery."
- Job placement targets are set annually per regional office based on the previous year's performance, and negotiated with individual providers.

([MCCSS 2024–2025 Service Objectives — job retention and advancement](http://www.ontario.ca/document/2024-2025-mccss-service-objectives-social-assistance/2024-2025-services-delivered-job-0); [ODSP policy directive 5.1, Employment supports funding](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-disability-support-program-policy-directives-employment-supports/51-employment); [ODSP policy directive 4.2, Job retention and advancement](https://www.ontario.ca/document/ontario-disability-support-program-policy-directives-employment-supports/42-job-retention))

**Employment Services Transformation / Integrated Employment Services:**

The model has the province as system steward overseeing third-party **Service System Managers (SSMs)** in **15 catchment areas**, links SSM funding to achievement of specific employment outcomes, and uses a province-wide **Common Assessment Tool (CAT)** to measure each job seeker's "distance from the labour market" and match them to services ([Maytree, Early signs of trouble](https://maytree.com/wp-content/uploads/Early-signs-of-trouble-EST-evaluation.pdf)).

The third-party Goss Gilroy evaluation of the three prototype catchments (Hamilton-Niagara, Peel, Muskoka-Kawarthas), finalized August 2023, was **not publicly released by the ministry** — Maytree obtained the full copy through a Freedom of Information request and found "the government's public summary of the report did not accurately reflect the full scope and context of the findings." Key numbers:

- **87% of clients who completed pre-employment services were employed 20 or more hours per week at some point** during the 12-month period — but this **"declined over time, starting at 86% upon exit, and reaching 62% by the 12-month point."**
- **Almost two-thirds of clients who completed pre-employment services met or exceeded their personal hourly wage goal**, with individuals making between **$18.70 and $19.70 per hour**.
- **IES clients had a 10% lower probability than clients in matched communities of completing their pre-employment services**, though they had comparatively higher employment rates after completing.
- Client mix in prototype catchments: **44% at high risk of long-term unemployment, 42% on social assistance, 48% racialized, 45% persons with disability, 29% youth with higher support needs, 18% newcomers, 3% Indigenous**.
- **Employer-side results were poor: only 26% of employers reported their ability to find workers with the right skills improved** after receiving Employer Supports, and **only 20% noted that their ability to retain workers increased**. (Two-thirds of employers surveyed did report having access to sufficient supports to accommodate jobseekers with disabilities.)
- On the 20-hour threshold: **"performance based funding dictates that SSMs are only paid if clients obtain jobs with at least an average of 20-hours/week… This resulted in the widely held perception, that people with the fewest barriers are getting the most support, rather than those who might face multiple barriers to employment."** Most stakeholder groups "reported that the threshold of a 20-hour work-week is not working well," and that SSMs "should be incentivized to help clients obtain jobs that are the best fit, rather than being focused on 'checking boxes'."
- **On administrative burden — commercially the most interesting line in the report:** "Another service provider group noted that they have **transformed an entire department to a 'retention department' that strictly captures proof of employment**, in order to accurately reflect this data for SSMs. This, in combination with other additional administrative duties, has increasingly detracted their group from providing quality services to their clients."
- The evaluators themselves cautioned that quantitative results are based on n = 1,254 completed surveys and that "IES clients who had more favourable experiences and employment outcomes were more motivated to participate in the survey," which "would conceivably lead to more upwardly biased estimates."

([Maytree, Early signs of trouble](https://maytree.com/wp-content/uploads/Early-signs-of-trouble-EST-evaluation.pdf); [ODEN mirror of the same document](https://www.odenetwork.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Early-signs-of-trouble-EST-evaluation.pdf); [full Goss Gilroy EST evaluation report](https://maytree.com/wp-content/uploads/Final-EST-Evaluation-Report.pdf))

**What exact outcome numbers a vendor needs to produce to be credible:**

1. Placement to **13 cumulative weeks** of employment.
2. Sustained **20+ hours per week**, measured at **3, 6 and 12 months** (and honestly reported against the known 86% → 62% decay curve).
3. **Hourly wage at or above ~$18.70**.
4. **Month-by-month retention** out to **15 months** (non-ODSP-income-support) or **33 months** (ODSP income support recipients).
5. For pre-apprenticeship: **80% program completion and 80% post-program transition** into registered apprenticeship or skilled trades employment.

### Q6. Does verified credentialing change hiring behaviour?

**Largely no, and the best available evidence is discouraging.**

**The strongest study on credential substitution.** The Burning Glass Institute / OneTen analysis, using a social profiles database representing 40% of the US workforce, found that **"a little more than one-third of major US firms that removed degree requirements from their job postings actually hired candidates without degrees."** Testing what happens when degree requirements are removed, they found "most companies do not directly swap one for the other" — firms that dropped degree requirements saw only a **2-percentage-point increase in the share of hires holding non-degree credentials**, "a modest but statistically positive shift." Their conclusion: **"Without broader organizational alignment, clear guidance for hiring managers on what credentials to look for, and systems that can actually recognize and value credentials, skills-first hiring is likely to remain largely aspirational"** ([Credential Fluency, OneTen / Burning Glass Institute](https://skillsright.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Credential-Fluency_The-Hiring-Advantage-in-the-Race-for-Skills_OneTen_BGI_Report.pdf)).

Practitioner analysis reaches the same verdict on mechanism: "Traditional filters first… Only once candidates clear all those informal pedigree gates do they see work samples, coding tests, case studies or task-based interviews. In other words, **skills are rarely used to widen the pool. They are used to narrow it at the end**" ([Employer Branding News](https://employerbranding.news/the-skills-based-hiring-con/)).

**The most direct causal test, and it is Canadian.** Bellemare, Goussé, Lacroix and Marchand's video résumé field experiment, published in the *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*: **"We find that revealing a disability decreases callback rates by 25 percentage points. This result is not explained by accessibility constraints or lower productivity due to disability. We find that including a video résumé of a well-spoken applicant significantly increases callbacks by 10 percentage points for persons with and without disabilities, suggesting that discrimination is unaffected by quality signals in our context."** Analysis of viewing activity suggested "employers seek less information when the applicant is disabled" ([AEJ: Applied Economics 15(4)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aejapp/v15y2023i4p452-76.html)).

**A high-fidelity, verified quality signal raised everyone's callbacks and did not close the disability gap.** That is close to a direct experimental refutation of "verification will change how employers treat this population."

**The closest existing analog — a work-readiness certificate.** The ACT National Career Readiness Certificate (NCRC), built on WorkKeys assessments and used by Missouri job centres since 2002 "as a way to evaluate work readiness":

- Individuals earning higher NCRC levels "were more likely to find employment, **though they did not necessarily find it faster**."
- **"Higher NCRC levels were not associated with shorter periods of unemployment. The mean unemployment claim duration for each NCRC level differed little from the overall average of 19.5 weeks."** The researchers' own candidate explanations included that "employers hiring more skilled workers were less likely to recognize the NCRC."
- ([Missouri/ACT evaluation](https://www.workreadycommunities.org/files/pdf/R1728-ncrc-mo-success-2018-11.pdf); [case study version with Figure 1 and Table 1](https://www.workreadycommunities.org/files/pdf/Case%20Studies%20WF%20&%20EDU%20outcomes%20NCRC.pdf))

ACT's income studies, matching 50,000 WorkKeys examinees to Equifax income records 2011–2016, show higher NCRC levels associated with higher incomes and faster income growth "outpacing national trends by a wide margin" ([ACT, Income Trends for NCRC Earners](https://www.act.org/content/dam/act/unsecured/documents/R1714-income-trends-ncrc-2018-08.pdf); [ACT, NCRC Outcomes](https://www.act.org/content/dam/act/unsecured/documents/NCRC-Outcomes.pdf)). **These are vendor-produced and correlational, not causal** — self-selection into testing is unaddressed, and ACT's own materials acknowledge "other potential factors."

### Q7. Barriers evidence — is "cannot prove readiness" genuinely a top barrier?

**No. The evidence points clearly elsewhere. This is the second-most important finding in this brief.**

**What people with disabilities actually report.** Statistics Canada's 2024 Survey Series on Accessibility — Experiences with Accessibility and Employment (SSA-EAE):

- **Nearly three in five (59%)** persons with disabilities or long-term conditions experienced a labour market-related barrier.
- **Nearly 7 in 10 (68%)** experienced a barrier during a hiring process, or one that discouraged or prevented them from working or seeking employment. The most common types were:
  - **Difficulties disclosing their disability to potential employers — 54%**
  - **Difficulties related to accommodations (unmet requests, unable to request, unaware accommodations were available) — 32%**
  - **Lack of support or respect from hiring staff or past colleagues/managers — 31%**
- Barriers at work: **physical environment 49%, communication 41%, transportation 35%, technology 35%**. Within physical environment, lighting or sound levels (31%) and sidewalks/pedestrian paths (11%) led.

([Statistics Canada, The Daily](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/250210/dq250210c-eng.htm); [PDF version](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/250210/dq250210c-eng.pdf))

**"Cannot prove I am ready" does not appear as a named barrier category. The top barrier is the opposite problem: disclosure is dangerous — and Trampoline is a disclosure instrument.**

**Accommodation.** **35.4% of employed Canadians with disabilities needed a workplace accommodation and 35.4% had an unmet need** in 2022 (both down from 2017). Top needs: modified work hours 16.3%, modified or different duties 11.6%, working from home 10.9%, modified/ergonomic workstation 10.7%, special chair or back support 10.3%. Workers with more severe disabilities, and those with cognitive, physical, or mental health-related disabilities, were more likely to have an unmet need ([Statistics Canada](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/o1/en/plus/7142-more-canadians-disabilities-workforce-unmet-accommodation-needs-among-barriers-equity); [underlying study PDF](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/36-28-0001/2024008/article/00004-eng.pdf)).

**Accommodation cost is rarely a valid legal defence in Ontario.** Under the Human Rights Code, costs must be "quantifiable, shown to be related to the accommodation, and so substantial that they would alter the essential nature of the enterprise, or so significant that they would substantially affect its viability." **"Factors such as business inconvenience, employee morale and customer and third-party preferences are not valid considerations."** The onus of proof is on the organization, not the person with a disability, and "mere speculation… will not generally be persuasive" ([Ontario Human Rights Commission, Undue hardship](http://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/policy-ableism-and-discrimination-based-disability/9-undue-hardship)).

**Discrimination is measured, large, and unresponsive to information.** The Quebec field experiment sent 1,477 applications to private firms in Montréal and Québec City advertising open positions:

- The IZA working paper reports baseline callback rates ranging 11.6%–17.4% across target positions, **averaging 14.4%**, with disclosure of a physical disability producing an average callback rate **7.2 percentage points lower** — a statistically significant difference.
- **"We estimate that a lower bound of the proportion of discriminating firms is 49.7%."**
- **"Mentioning eligibility to a government subsidy to cover the cost of workplace adaptation does not increase callback rates."**
- The differential "does not result from accessibility constraints related to firm infrastructures."

([IZA Discussion Paper 11461](https://docs.iza.org/dp11461.pdf); [SSRN version, which reports the comparison as 14.4% vs 31%, a 46% differential — note the working-paper versions differ and should be reconciled against the published article before citing](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3175365))

Comparable international results: in Denmark, applicants without a disability received more than twice as many interview invitations as wheelchair users (**17.0% vs 7.7%**); in Norway, a 48% difference; in Canada, Bellemare et al. found applicants without a disability received twice as many invitations "despite similar credentials" ([Scandinavian Journal of Disability Research](https://sjdr.se/articles/10.16993/sjdr.944)). A Canadian matched-pair audit study across Toronto and Edmonton found small overall differences but "considerably more noticeable" effects **among labourer jobs**, regardless of whether the applicant signalled a physical or cognitive disability ([Pettinicchio seminar abstract](https://www.stat.unipd.it/sites/stat.unipd.it/files/Pettinicchio_abstract.pdf)).

**Two independent facts here — subsidies don't move employers, and quality signals don't move employers — should make you very cautious about a product whose theory of change is "give employers better information."**

**The benefits cliff likely dominates the person's incentive.**

Current ODSP rules: a person with a disability may earn **up to $1,000/month in net employment income with no reduction** in income support. **Above $1,000, ODSP deducts an amount equal to three-quarters (75%) of net monthly earnings** — only 25% of earnings above $1,000 is exempt. A **$100/month Work-Related Benefit** is available in each month with employment earnings. Worked example: at $1,500 net monthly earnings, ODSP subtracts $375 ([Ontario.ca](https://www.ontario.ca/page/working-and-earning-ontario-disability-support-program); [CLEO](https://www.cleo.on.ca/wp-content/uploads/workodsp.pdf); [Steps to Justice](https://stepstojustice.ca/steps/income-assistance/2-learn-how-earnings-exemptions-affect-your-odsp/)).

The February 2023 change raised the flat exemption from **$200 to $1,000** but simultaneously raised the withdrawal rate from **50% to 75%**. ISAC's analysis:

- The changes **"improve the incomes of people on ODSP who earn up to $2600 net… each month"** and have **"no impact"** on those who cannot work.
- **"The proposed rules will mean that people become ineligible for ODSP at a lower income point. They will 'earn their way off' the program $224 earlier per year ($18.67 per month). At that point, they will no longer have access to associated supports that cover some disability costs such as prescription drug coverage, assistive living devices, or the Guide Dog Benefit."**

([Income Security Advocacy Centre](https://incomesecurity.org/impact-of-proposed-changes-to-earned-income-exemptions-for-odsp-november-2022/))

Maytree's analysis of the same change: "the proposed system gives ODSP claimants a stronger incentive to earn on average $6,000 a year, but **a weaker incentive to earn anything above that**" ([Maytree](https://maytree.com/publications/how-do-earnings-exemptions-affect-social-assistance-recipients-in-ontario/)).

On losing ODSP: a person who stops receiving ODSP because of earnings "might be able to get" the Extended Health Benefit, an Employment Transition Benefit of **$500**, or the Transitional Health Benefit ([CLEO](https://www.cleo.on.ca/wp-content/uploads/workodsp.pdf)).

**The behavioural consequence is documented and severe:**

- Ontario research on work integration social enterprises found workers **"hesitated to increase their hours of work, even if they were capable of doing so at the time, for fear that this could flag them for review and ultimately be deemed ineligible for ODSP."** A supervisor quoted: *"A lot of people will not work full-time because they're terrified of losing… I'll underwork, underperform because I'm terrified of not having that social safety net."* The authors note ODSP's structure "is based on assumptions about disability as being chronic and/or permanent, and as a result, is not well aligned with episodic disabilities" ([Journal of Disability Policy Studies](https://sage.cnpereading.com/doi/10.1177/10442073261430840)).
- A grounded-theory study of ODSP work exits is titled **"The Ontario Disability Support Program Work Exit Process: Parallels to a Hostage Negotiation."** Its main finding: **"system supports are not the determining factors in a successful transition. Rather, participants describe how recipients exit for employment by leveraging personal resources"** ([Journal of Disability Policy Studies](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1044207320944609)).
- Advocacy research documents recipients "living in a state of anxiety and fear, in a poverty trap," and one participant who "cut down my contract hours so as not to face losing ODSP benefits." Once work begins, "a complex, difficult and unresponsive system of monthly reporting and reconciliation begins with failure to meet all requirements routinely resulting in ineligibility where all benefits are suspended or cancelled" ([CRWDP, What stops us from working? Ten years later](https://www.crwdp.ca/sites/default/files/documentuploader/what_stops_us_from_working_10_years_later-v.7-20211123.pdf)).
- CAMH's earlier report found **"Eighty-nine percent of ODSP recipients are unemployed"** and **"Only 11% of ODSP recipients work, earning an average of $650 per month"** — making recipients "11 times more likely to be unemployed than the average Ontarian." It also notes some recipients work "under the table… without risking the loss of benefits." **This figure predates the 2023 rule change and should be treated as dated** ([CAMH, What stops us from working?](https://www.camh.ca/-/media/files/pdfs---public-policy-submissions/odsp-report-final-pdf.pdf)).

**And this collides directly with Employment Ontario's design.** The EST evaluation records "challenges faced by people with disabilities in meeting the requirement of working 20 hours per week, **with some noting that recipients have concerns about losing financial supports**" ([Maytree](https://maytree.com/wp-content/uploads/Early-signs-of-trouble-EST-evaluation.pdf)). A person on ODSP working 20 hours/week at $18.70 earns roughly $1,600/month gross — well into the 75% clawback zone.

**The funder's outcome metric and the client's financial self-interest are in direct opposition, and no readiness passport fixes that.**

### Q8. Published evaluations of readiness gating or staged-unlock interventions

**No strong evidence found.** I could not locate a single published evaluation of a "readiness passport," staged-unlock credential, partner-attestation gating system, or equivalent, with measured employment outcomes. The nearest analogs, in descending order of relevance:

1. **Prevocational training** — evaluated repeatedly in RCTs and found ineffective relative to rapid placement, and statistically indistinguishable from psychiatric care alone (see Q1).
2. **The ACT NCRC** — associational evidence only; no effect on unemployment duration (see Q6).
3. **Ontario's own Common Assessment Tool** within EST, which segments clients by "distance from the labour market" and matches them to services. The evaluation records "initial concerns about the Common Assessment Tool, the Segmentation Model, PBF," alongside the finding that the incentive structure "puts greater emphasis on those who are most employable" ([full evaluation](https://maytree.com/wp-content/uploads/Final-EST-Evaluation-Report.pdf); [summary](https://maytree.com/wp-content/uploads/Early-signs-of-trouble-EST-evaluation.pdf)). **Ontario already has a readiness-staging instrument, and its published evaluation is unflattering.**

**One adjacent Ontario cost-benefit model exists**, though it is a *projection*, not a measured result: if 100 individuals moving from high school to employment attended Project SEARCH and an estimated 70% obtained "moderate outcomes," the model estimates a **lifetime net benefit of $8,913,694.31** (including investment for those who did not connect to the labour market); the corresponding Employment Pathways scenario estimates **$10,025,046.95**. The model was updated to align with current Integrated Employment Services funding and was robust to sensitivity analyses ([Holland Bloorview, Funding Employment Inclusion for Ontario Youth with Disabilities](https://hollandbloorview.ca/sites/default/files/2024-09/Funding-Employment-Inclusion-2024_0.pdf)).

**A further problem specific to Trampoline's attestation layer.** The clinical assessments Trampoline would aggregate have contested predictive validity:

- A systematic review of FCE methods includes studies titled **"Functional Capacity Evaluation Performance Does Not Predict Sustained Return to Work in Claimants With Chronic Back Pain"** (Gross & Battié) and **"A Short-Form Functional Capacity Evaluation Predicts Time to Recovery but Not Sustained Return-to-Work"** (Branton et al.) ([J Occup Rehabil](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10926-017-9734-x)).
- A prospective cohort of 220 rehabilitation patients with chronic musculoskeletal disorders (145 with complete data; 37.9% non-return-to-work at 1-year follow-up) found all FCE information significantly related to RTW (r = .28–.43), **"but the models' quality was low. The integration of FCE information led to an increase of 5%. The predictive efficiency was poor"** ([Arch Phys Med Rehabil](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apmr.2008.08.218)).
- Commentators argue the construct problem is fundamental: **"It is critical to understand that an instrument measuring a single dimension cannot be expected to assess a multidimensional construct. It is, therefore, by definition incorrect to suggest or to claim that the results of an FCE should be able to predict a person's work ability."** At best an FCE measures "immediate functional ability to perform physical work-related activities" ([WORK](https://doi.org/10.3233/wor-2009-0835)).

**In fairness, this is genuinely mixed rather than uniformly negative.** A systematic review of 18 studies found **"Strong evidence exists that a number of performance-based measures are predictive of work participation in patients with MSDs, especially lifting tests"** (15 of 18 studies reported performance-based measures were predictive) ([Int Arch Occup Environ Health](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00420-011-0659-y.pdf)). And a review of functional tests for non-specific low back pain identified the Back Performance Scale and back endurance test as "most promising," while concluding that **"The question of when LBP patients can resume everyday activities and work is not possible to determine with functional tests alone. Psychosocial aspects and work demands must be considered"** ([PMC10049133](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10049133/)).

**Ontario delivery context.** IPS is described by CMHA Ontario as "a noted best practice in employment services for persons with mental health conditions, and **the dominant model** for employment support," whose goal "is to support people to move into competitive employment **as soon as possible**" ([CMHA Ontario, Short Report on Supported Employment Programs](https://ontario.cmha.ca/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/CMHA-Ontario-Employment-Support-Scan_042012.pdf)). CMHA's employment forum proceedings note that outside of IPS providers, "a shared and comprehensive approach to program evaluation is not done," with most evaluation consisting of consumer satisfaction surveys ([CMHA Ontario Employment Forum 2010](https://ontario.cmha.ca/wp-content/uploads/2010/09/cmha_on_proceedings_employment_forum_2010.pdf)). Both documents are dated and predate EST.

## 3. Strongest evidence against this product's core thesis

Stated as forcefully as the evidence permits. This section is not hedged.

**1. The gating premise is the exact thing that ~28 RCTs were run to disprove.** Pre-vocational preparation did not merely underperform rapid placement (34% vs 12% employed at 18 months) — it **failed to beat standard community care** ([Cochrane CD003080](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD003080_vocational-rehabilitation-people-severe-mental-illness)), and in the network meta-analysis was **statistically indistinguishable from psychiatric care alone** (RR 1.26, 95% CI 0.73–2.19) ([Cochrane CD011867](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD011867_helping-adults-severe-mental-illness-get-job-and-keep-it-network-meta-analysis)). Any funder with a research advisor will know this literature. Selling staged gating into Ontario's mental health and disability employment system means selling directly against its own stated best practice ([CMHA Ontario](https://ontario.cmha.ca/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/CMHA-Ontario-Employment-Support-Scan_042012.pdf)).

**2. Professional readiness judgment has been tested and found to lack predictive power.** **"Practitioners are unable to predict who will be successful at work"** ([IPS manual](https://www.umassmed.edu/contentassets/32a28fea786c4a62a8f3e47242ebaf1e/ips-se-for-youth_print-prepub-final.pdf)); job readiness screening is explicitly excluded from best practice ([SJDR](https://sjdr.se/articles/10.16993/sjdr.1008)); and people with no expressed interest in work succeed at comparable rates when simply given services ([PMC4636005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4636005/)). Trampoline's entire value rests on aggregating exactly the judgments this literature says do not predict — and the clinical instruments underneath show **"poor" predictive efficiency** for sustained return to work, adding roughly 5% to model quality ([Arch Phys Med Rehabil](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apmr.2008.08.218)).

**3. Better information about candidates does not change employer behaviour toward this population.** In a Canadian field experiment, a high-quality verified signal (a video résumé of a well-spoken applicant) raised callbacks by **10 percentage points for everyone and left the 25-percentage-point disability gap intact** — "discrimination is unaffected by quality signals in our context" ([AEJ: Applied](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aejapp/v15y2023i4p452-76.html)). Separately, telling employers the **government would pay** for workplace adaptation did not increase callbacks either, with **at least 49.7% of firms discriminating** ([IZA](https://docs.iza.org/dp11461.pdf)). If money doesn't move them and quality signals don't move them, a readiness verdict is unlikely to.

**4. Employers who adopt skills-first hiring on paper mostly do not change who they hire.** Only **a little more than one-third** of major firms that dropped degree requirements actually hired non-degree candidates, and the shift toward non-degree credentials was **2 percentage points** ([Burning Glass Institute / OneTen](https://skillsright.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Credential-Fluency_The-Hiring-Advantage-in-the-Race-for-Skills_OneTen_BGI_Report.pdf)). Trampoline's "employers publish requirements as gates" feature assumes an employer behaviour that has been measured and found largely absent — and where skills evidence is used, it narrows the pool at the end rather than widening it ([Employer Branding News](https://employerbranding.news/the-skills-based-hiring-con/)).

**5. The barrier being solved is not the barrier people report.** The top hiring-stage barrier is **difficulty disclosing disability (54%)**, followed by accommodation problems (32%) and lack of support/respect from hiring staff (31%) — not proving capability ([Statistics Canada](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/250210/dq250210c-eng.htm)). Trampoline is structurally a disclosure system: even storing only verdicts and restrictions, a "restricted" verdict or a "Stabilize" level communicates health information to an employer. **There is a real risk the product increases the exposure that people identify as their single biggest problem.**

**6. The dominant financial incentive runs the other way.** A **75% clawback above $1,000/month net**, plus loss of prescription drug, dental and assistive device coverage on exit ([Ontario.ca](https://www.ontario.ca/page/working-and-earning-ontario-disability-support-program); [ISAC](https://incomesecurity.org/impact-of-proposed-changes-to-earned-income-exemptions-for-odsp-november-2022/)), produces documented, deliberate underworking ([JDPS](https://sage.cnpereading.com/doi/10.1177/10442073261430840); [CRWDP](https://www.crwdp.ca/sites/default/files/documentuploader/what_stops_us_from_working_10_years_later-v.7-20211123.pdf)). If a client rationally does not want the 20-hour job, no amount of verified readiness produces the outcome the funder pays for. And research on ODSP work exits concludes that **"system supports are not the determining factors in a successful transition"** ([JDPS](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1044207320944609)).

**7. Ontario ran a version of this experiment already, and it went badly for the hardest cases.** Segmentation-by-distance-from-labour-market plus outcome-gated funding produced the widely held perception that **"people with the fewest barriers are getting the most support, rather than those who might face multiple barriers to employment,"** and stakeholders reported providers "checking boxes" rather than seeking best fit ([Maytree / Goss Gilroy](https://maytree.com/wp-content/uploads/Early-signs-of-trouble-EST-evaluation.pdf)). Adding a second, finer-grained gating layer plausibly worsens creaming rather than fixing it.

**8. The historical objection to prevocational models applies to Trampoline verbatim.** The Cochrane review records that critics of pre-vocational training argued it **"promoted dependency and deterred… developing work skills"** ([Cochrane full review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4170889/)). A five-stage ladder with expiring attestations and a "90-day Hold" state is, structurally, a machine for generating waiting — in a domain where **after 12 weeks off work the risk of prolonged absence rises dramatically, and by 6 months only 50% ever return to full-time employment** ([WSIB](https://www.wsib.ca/sites/default/files/2022-11/betteratwork.pdf)).

## 4. What the evidence DOES support about this design

Not nothing — and several of these are strong.

**Healthcare-provider-to-workplace contact is one of only two interventions with *strong* evidence for reducing work disability duration**, alongside work accommodation offers; early contact with the worker and a return-to-work coordinator carry *moderate* evidence, as does the cost reduction from all five components ([IWH](https://www.iwh.on.ca/summaries/sharing-best-evidence/workplace-based-return-to-work-interventions); [J Occup Rehabil](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10926-005-8038-8)). Trampoline's partner network is, structurally, infrastructure for exactly that. **Reposition the attestation as a communication channel between clinician and workplace, not as a gate.** This is the single highest-value repositioning available.

**Restrictions and expiry dates map onto the accommodation problem, which is real, large and unmet.** **35.4% of employed Canadians with disabilities have an unmet accommodation need**, concentrated among those with more severe, cognitive, physical or mental health-related disabilities ([Statistics Canada](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/o1/en/plus/7142-more-canadians-disabilities-workforce-unmet-accommodation-needs-among-barriers-equity)). Ontario law places the burden of proving undue hardship on the employer and rules out inconvenience, morale and customer preference as defences ([OHRC](http://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/policy-ableism-and-discrimination-based-disability/9-undue-hardship)). A structured, portable record of *restrictions* is an accommodation-specification tool with a documented evidence base. "Readiness certification" does not have one.

**Individualized routing — as distinct from universal gating — has the best empirical support in the welfare literature.** MDRC found the most effective programs were mixed-strategy, with some participants starting in job search and others in training, indicating **"a more individualized approach may be most promising,"** while warning that "the basis on which people are assigned to services" is critical ([MDRC](https://www.mdrc.org/work/publications/how-welfare-and-work-policies-affect-employment-and-income)). Trampoline as a *matching and triage engine* that routes most people straight to placement and only a minority to stabilization is defensible. Trampoline as a ladder everyone must climb is not.

**A real, funded, current administrative pain exists — and it may be the actual product.** An Ontario provider **"transformed an entire department to a 'retention department' that strictly captures proof of employment,"** which "has increasingly detracted their group from providing quality services to their clients" ([Maytree](https://maytree.com/wp-content/uploads/Early-signs-of-trouble-EST-evaluation.pdf)). Outcome verification and evidence capture — proving 13 cumulative weeks, 20 hours/week, wage levels, and month-by-month retention for up to **33 months** ([MCCSS](http://www.ontario.ca/document/2024-2025-mccss-service-objectives-social-assistance/2024-2025-services-delivered-job-0)) — is a genuine cost centre today, with money already flowing against it.

**The timing and expiry logic is sound even where the gating logic is not.** After 12 weeks off work the risk of prolonged absence rises sharply, and by 6 months only 50% ever return to full-time work ([WSIB, Better at Work](https://www.wsib.ca/sites/default/files/2022-11/betteratwork.pdf)). Expiry dates create urgency in a domain where urgency is genuinely protective. The design requirement is that expiry pushes people **forward**, not out.

**Attrition is real and large enough to be worth money.** ~47% Ontario apprenticeship completion, 35% in voluntary trades ([Auditor General](https://auditor.on.ca/en/content/annualreports/arreports/en16/v1_304en16.pdf)), against a contractual **80%/80%** pre-apprenticeship target ([MLITSD](https://forms.mgcs.gov.on.ca/en/dataset/1c808860-adfb-451b-a3fe-3e9a82681e18/resource/4dfa3831-e533-4d0a-9983-45caa0ec4e0b/download/pre-apprenticeship-2026-27-guidelines-en-final.pdf)) is a large, funded, quantified gap that someone is accountable for closing.

**Process redesign in this system demonstrably moves large amounts of money.** WSIB's NSDM cut 12-month benefit persistence from 8.9% to 3.9% and coincided with a **$181 million** reduction in loss-of-earnings benefits; the Work Reintegration Program roughly doubled return-to-work rates (36% → 69%) while cutting program cost from **$168M to $91M** ([IWH](https://www.iwh.on.ca/impact-case-studies/evidence-based-service-delivery-model-at-wsib-improved-return-to-work-outcomes); [IWH](https://www.iwh.on.ca/impact-case-studies/wsib-work-reintegration-program-improves-outcomes-lowers-costs)). The buyer has a demonstrated history of paying for coordination improvements.

## 6. A concrete, cheap pilot that would generate proof

**The design constraint.** The core claim is *causal* ("gating reduces dropout/no-shows"), the literature's prior is *against* it, and budget is limited. Therefore the pilot needs randomization, not a before/after comparison — a pre/post story will be dismissed as a selection effect by anyone who knows this field. Note that even the government's own EST evaluators flagged that survey-based outcomes were "conceivably… upwardly biased" because more successful clients were more motivated to respond ([Maytree](https://maytree.com/wp-content/uploads/Early-signs-of-trouble-EST-evaluation.pdf)). Do not repeat that mistake.

### Where to run it

One Ontario **pre-apprenticeship cohort** or one Service System Manager's caseload. **Pre-apprenticeship is the better first pilot:** the 80%/80% target is contractual, cohorts are naturally sized and time-boxed, completion is unambiguously defined by the ministry, and the funder **already collects 3-, 6- and 12-month post-program follow-up** ([MLITSD](https://forms.mgcs.gov.on.ca/en/dataset/1c808860-adfb-451b-a3fe-3e9a82681e18/resource/4dfa3831-e533-4d0a-9983-45caa0ec4e0b/download/pre-apprenticeship-2026-27-guidelines-en-final.pdf)). You inherit the measurement infrastructure for free.

### Design

Randomize at the **individual level within a single provider** — Trampoline-enabled versus the provider's usual process. Individual randomization inside one site controls for provider quality, local labour market and cohort effects, and avoids the cost of recruiting multiple sites.

### Why it tests routing rather than gating

**Do not run an arm in which a low readiness level blocks someone from a seat or a job.** That arm is ethically exposed, is inconsistent with the zero-exclusion standard the sector's best-practice model is built on ([MDRC](https://www.mdrc.org/work/publications/individual-placement-and-support/file-full)), and — on the strength of the Cochrane evidence — is the arm most likely to lose.

Instead, the treatment arm uses Trampoline attestations to **trigger support and specify accommodations**, with placement available to everyone regardless of readiness level. Concretely, an attestation with restrictions should fire a clinician-to-workplace contact and an accommodation offer — the two components with **strong** evidence for reducing work disability duration ([IWH](https://www.iwh.on.ca/summaries/sharing-best-evidence/workplace-based-return-to-work-interventions)) — rather than a hold.

This makes the pilot a test of MDRC's "middle of the spectrum" finding — that individualized assignment beats uniform strategy, provided the basis of assignment is sound ([MDRC](https://www.mdrc.org/work/publications/how-welfare-and-work-policies-affect-employment-and-income)). **If this arm wins, you have a product consistent with the evidence base rather than at war with it. If you test gating and win, reviewers will not believe you; if you test gating and lose, you have spent the budget confirming the prior.**

### Primary outcome

Pre-register **one**:

- **Sustained 20+ hours/week at 12 months** — matches where the funder's money actually is; or
- **Program completion per the MLITSD definition** — participants enrolled on day 1 who complete all mandatory program activities.

### Secondary outcomes

- Attestation-to-placement lag (days).
- No-show rate to first scheduled shift or class.
- **13-cumulative-week placement rate** (the ODSP Employment Supports payment trigger).
- Wage at placement, against the **$18.70/hr** benchmark.
- Days absent during the program.
- Accommodation requests made, and accommodation requests met.
- **Administrative hours spent capturing proof of employment** — measured in both arms. Given the "retention department" finding ([Maytree](https://maytree.com/wp-content/uploads/Early-signs-of-trouble-EST-evaluation.pdf)), this may be where the defensible ROI actually lives, and it is cheap to measure.

### Sample size

Ontario pre-apprenticeship targets 80% completion; a provider running at roughly 65% would need an improvement of about 15 percentage points to be interesting. **Detecting a 15-percentage-point difference in a binary outcome at 80% power and 5% significance requires roughly 150 per arm (~300 total)** — likely 3–6 cohorts.

Two ways to make this tractable on a small budget:

1. **Power on a continuous outcome instead** — days to placement, or hours worked per week at 12 months — which buys meaningful power at substantially smaller n than a binary completion endpoint.
2. **If only ~120 participants total are achievable, pre-register the study explicitly as a feasibility pilot**, with progression criteria (recruitment rate, attestation capture rate, follow-up rate) as the primary endpoints and effect estimates reported with confidence intervals as secondary. **Reporting an underpowered null honestly is far better for long-term credibility than overclaiming from n = 60.**

### Duration

**12 months minimum**, with measurement at 3, 6 and 12 months to match MLITSD's existing follow-up schedule. The EST data shows outcomes look excellent at exit (86%) and degrade badly by month 12 (62%) — **a 3-month readout will flatter the product and then be contradicted by the funder's own data.**

### Two things to instrument that will decide the product's fate

1. **ODSP earnings-clawback exposure for every participant.** Record how many are near or above the **$1,000/month** net threshold and whether any deliberately cap hours. If clawback avoidance dominates outcome variance, that finding is more commercially valuable than the pilot's headline result, because it tells you the product cannot deliver the funder's metric no matter how well the attestation layer works ([Ontario.ca](https://www.ontario.ca/page/working-and-earning-ontario-disability-support-program); [ISAC](https://incomesecurity.org/impact-of-proposed-changes-to-earned-income-exemptions-for-odsp-november-2022/)).
2. **A small employer-side signalling test.** Present matched candidate profiles with and without the Trampoline verdict to real hiring managers and measure interview rates. The Bellemare et al. video-résumé result predicts **no differential effect** ([AEJ: Applied](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aejapp/v15y2023i4p452-76.html)). If you find one, it is a genuinely novel and highly saleable finding. If you do not, you have cheaply saved yourself from building the employer-gate feature.

### What to do with a null

Given the prior literature, a null on gating is the most likely outcome. The second and third pilots most worth running are:

- **Trampoline as retention-evidence infrastructure**, sold to SSMs on administrative cost per verified outcome (grounded in the "retention department" finding).
- **Trampoline as a clinician-to-workplace accommodation channel**, sold on days-of-absence to WSIB-exposed employers (grounded in the IWH strong-evidence components and WSIB's demonstrated willingness to pay for duration reduction).

Both align with interventions that already have evidence behind them, and both reuse the same partner-attestation infrastructure.

## 7. Evidence gaps and weak sources

Stated plainly, so nothing here is accidentally over-claimed later.

### Where I found nothing at all

- **No published evaluation of any readiness-passport, staged-unlock, or partner-attestation gating intervention** with measured employment outcomes, anywhere. Trampoline's specific mechanism is unevaluated. The nearest analogs (prevocational training, the ACT NCRC, Ontario's Common Assessment Tool) all return neutral-to-negative results.
- **No province-wide Ontario no-show statistic** for employment programs, pre-apprenticeship, or training. The product brief assumes no-shows are a quantified problem; in Ontario they do not appear to be publicly quantified. Do not cite a no-show rate.
- **No evidence that verified third-party credentials change hiring decisions for people with disabilities.** The one direct Canadian causal test found quality signals do **not** close the disability callback gap.
- **No Canadian evidence on employer willingness to pay** for third-party readiness verification.

### Where the only sources are vendor blogs or marketing content

- **Q3 (turnover and cost-of-hire) almost in its entirety.** [Trimax](https://trimaxemployment.ca/warehouse-staffing-canada-guide/) and [Trimax](https://trimaxemployment.ca/real-cost-of-a-bad-hire-light-industrial-canada/) are staffing-agency marketing; [Canuck Hire](https://canuckhire.com/blog/employee-turnover-costs-canadian-smbs), [FirstHR](https://firsthr.app/blog/onboarding/cost-of-employee-turnover) and [G&A Partners](https://www.gnapartners.com/resources/articles/how-much-does-employee-turnover-really-cost-your-business) are HR-vendor content. None is primary research. All ultimately cite SHRM, Gallup, Mercer or the US Department of Labor without linking to the underlying study. **The SHRM, Gallup and Mercer figures should be sourced directly before use.**
- **The ACT NCRC income studies** ([ACT](https://www.act.org/content/dam/act/unsecured/documents/R1714-income-trends-ncrc-2018-08.pdf); [ACT](https://www.act.org/content/dam/act/unsecured/documents/NCRC-Outcomes.pdf)) are produced by the credential's own vendor, are correlational, and do not address self-selection into testing.
- Two Q6 sources — [Employer Branding News](https://employerbranding.news/the-skills-based-hiring-con/) and [CourseCareers](https://coursecareers.com/blog-posts/why-employers-no-longer-require-college-degrees) — are commentary/marketing, not research. The Burning Glass Institute report is the only rigorous source in that section and should carry the argument alone.
- [Skills-Based Hiring and Entrepreneurial Pathways](https://www.ias-research.com/research/research-journal/skills-based-hiring-and-entrepreneurial-pathways-india-us-canada) and [newcomersjobs.com](https://www.newcomersjobs.com/blog/why-skills-based-hiring-is-becoming-the-new-standard-for-canadian-employers) make assertive claims about Canadian skills-based hiring adoption with **no visible underlying data**. They are listed in Sources for completeness but **no claim in this brief rests on them.**

### Where the benchmark is US-only and may not transfer

- All cost-per-hire, time-to-fill, cost-of-turnover and cost-of-bad-hire figures (SHRM ~$4,700–$5,475; 44-day time to fill; Gallup 0.5x–2x salary; US DOL 30% of first-year earnings) are **US benchmarks applied to Canadian roles by third parties**.
- The **NEWWS / MDRC** welfare-to-work evidence is US, from 1991–1994 assignment cohorts, in a different benefits regime. The ~$300/year LFA advantage is not directly transferable to Ontario dollar terms.
- The **Burning Glass Institute / OneTen** degree-requirement analysis is US labour market data.
- Much of the **IPS RCT evidence** is US and European; Canadian IPS-specific outcome data was not located in this search.

### Where figures are dated and must be re-verified

- **CAMH's "only 11% of ODSP recipients work, earning an average of $650 per month"** ([CAMH](https://www.camh.ca/-/media/files/pdfs---public-policy-submissions/odsp-report-final-pdf.pdf)) **predates the February 2023 earnings-exemption change** and is likely a decade or more old. It is the most quotable statistic in the brief and the most likely to be challenged. Re-verify before any external use.
- The **Auditor General's 47% apprenticeship completion** figure covers **2011/12–2015/16** from a 2016 report. Current rates may differ materially.
- **HEQCO completion rates by trade** derive from the **2015 National Apprenticeship Survey**, which HEQCO itself notes "is not longitudinal" and may understate completion because some recorded discontinuers may complete later.
- The **Waterloo Region OW recidivism figures (52% / 69%)** are from **2011** and are a single region.
- The **IWH systematic review** searched literature to **December 2003** and included 10 studies. Its conclusions are widely cited but the evidence base is over two decades old.
- The **WSIB New Service Delivery Model** figures are from a **2013** WSIB strategic-plan report, and WSIB itself attributed part of the benefit reduction to a decline in accepted lost-time claims rather than to the intervention — **do not present the $181M as a clean causal effect.**
- **CMHA Ontario** documents are from **2010 and 2012** and predate Employment Services Transformation entirely.
- The **EST prototype evaluation** covers a **COVID-affected period** — the ministry's stated reason for not publishing it. This cuts both ways and should be acknowledged rather than hidden when citing the 86%→62% curve.

### Internal inconsistencies I could not resolve

- The **Bellemare et al. Quebec field experiment** appears in multiple versions with **materially different reported callback rates**: the [IZA working paper](https://docs.iza.org/dp11461.pdf) describes a 14.4% baseline with a 7.2-percentage-point reduction on disclosure, while the [SSRN version](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3175365) reports 14.4% vs 31% (a 46% differential), and the published [AEJ: Applied](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aejapp/v15y2023i4p452-76.html) video-résumé paper reports a 25-percentage-point reduction. **These are different studies and/or different versions. Cite the published AEJ article and verify the exact figure against it before use.**
- **FCE predictive validity is genuinely contested, not settled.** Gross & Battié found FCE does not predict sustained return to work and one cohort found "poor" predictive efficiency, while [Kuijer et al.](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00420-011-0659-y.pdf) found "strong evidence" that performance-based measures, especially lifting tests, *are* predictive of work participation. This brief reports both. Do not cite only the favourable side.
- The [2022 meta-analysis of work-related RTW interventions](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13643-022-02055-7) found **no significant benefit** of multidisciplinary rehabilitation over usual care (RR 1.01), which sits in tension with the IWH systematic review's positive findings. The honest reading is that *specific components* (accommodation offer, provider-workplace contact) have better support than *programs* as a whole.

### Methodological caveats attached to headline numbers

- The **EST 87% / 86%→62% figures** are based on **n = 1,254 survey respondents**, and the evaluators explicitly warned results "be used with caution due to concerns with bias, sample size, and recall error," noting likely upward bias from differential response.
- The **Cochrane network meta-analysis** results are based on **"moderate- to very low-quality evidence,"** and the authors state "future studies with lower risk of bias could change these results." The prevocational-training-vs-psychiatric-care-only comparison specifically rests on **very low-quality evidence**.
- The **Holland Bloorview $8.9M / $10.0M lifetime net benefit** figures are **modelled projections, not measured outcomes**.
